## Stage 1: Hook
**Objective:** Engage the learner with a Canada-relevant real-world story that introduces core ecosystem concepts

*Story: The Caribou and the Forest*

Deep in the heart of Canada's boreal forest, caribou are struggling to survive. The forests are changing due to new construction, logging, and climate change. A local scientist, Dr. Sarah, visits a caribou herd and notices that many young caribou are not growing well. She observes that the trees that caribou typically eat have become scarce. As she hikes through the forest, she discovers a new type of plant growing where there was once old-growth pine. Curious, she studies how the caribou's food source is changing, and what impact it has on the entire forest ecosystem.

Questions:
- Why might the caribou be struggling to grow?
- What does Dr. Sarah think might be happening in the forest?
- Is the caribou part of a food chain? What other animals are likely in that chain?
- Why might a new plant species grow in place of old-growth trees?

**Delivery notes:** This story introduces the importance of plant diversity, consumer relationships, and environmental adaptation in ecosystems.

## Stage 2: Concept
**Objective:** Teach the foundational vocabulary and relationships in ecosystems

**Vocabulary:** Producers, consumers, decomposers, food chain, food web, predator, prey, energy flow

*Concept: The Foundation of Ecosystems*

Within every ecosystem, there are living things that depend on each other to survive. These relationships form the foundation of how energy flows in nature.

1. **Producers** are the foundation of many food chains. They make their own food using sunlight and nutrients from the air and soil. Examples: trees, grasses, algae, and phytoplankton.

2. **Consumers** are organisms that get their energy by eating other living things. Consumers can be categorized into:
   - **Herbivores** – eat only plants.
   - **Carnivores** – eat other animals.
   - **Omnivores** – eat both plants and animals.

3. **Decomposers** break down dead organisms and waste, returning nutrients to the soil. Examples: fungi, bacteria, earthworms.

4. **Energy Flow**: The sun provides energy for producers to make food. As consumers eat producers (and other consumers), energy is passed along. Each level in a food chain loses energy, which is why there are usually fewer organisms at each level.

5. **Food Chains and Webs**: A food chain shows one path of energy flow. A food web shows all the connected food chains in an ecosystem.

6. **Predator and Prey**: Predators hunt and eat prey. Prey are animals that are hunted. This relationship keeps populations balanced in nature.

7. **Human Impact**: Human activities can change the natural balance of ecosystems. For example, cutting down too many trees reduces habitat for caribou, leading to fewer of them surviving.

Comprehension Check: 

What role do the leaves of a tree play in the ecosystem?
A) They are decomposers
B) They are producers  
C) They are nutrients
D) They are consumers

**Intervention Hints:**
- Level 1: Ask: "Can you think of a tree that makes food for itself using sunlight?" 
- Level 2: Explain the difference between 'food' and 'nutrients' in plants.
- Level 3: Relate the concept to another example like grass or algae.

**Answer: B) They are producers**

## Stage 3: Guided
**Objective:** Use structured support to help the learner solve ecosystem-related problems

**Problem: The Great Lakes and Lake Trout**

The Great Lakes ecosystem is home to lake trout, which are a top predator in the food chain. Fishermen often catch lake trout because they taste great. However, people are concerned that the population of lake trout may be declining due to pollution and habitat loss.

The following animals are found in Lake Superior and the Great Lakes: 

1. Phytoplankton
2. Zooplankton
3. Lake trout (a big fish)
4. Small fish like minnows

Which of these animals plays a role of a **producer** in this ecosystem?

A) Phytoplankton
B) Lake trout  
C) Zooplankton
D) Small fish like minnows

**Intervention Hints:**
- Level 1: Ask: "Which of these animals makes its own food using sunlight?"
- Level 2: Explain phytoplankton as tiny plants in water.
- Level 3: Ask students to think of other examples of producers (plants, algae, etc.)

**Answer: A) Phytoplankton**

**Guided Problem:**

**Problem: The Grassland Animal Family**

In a prairie grassland ecosystem, one family of animals includes:

1. Grass (producer)
2. Rabbit (herbivore)
3. Fox (carnivore)
4. Mushrooms (decomposer)

If the population of rabbits suddenly drops due to a disease, what might happen?

A) The foxes will have more food to eat
B) The grass will grow more
C) The mushrooms will decline
D) The foxes will die out

**Intervention Hints:**
- Level 1: Ask: "If rabbits disappear, what happens to the grass that they eat?"
- Level 2: Explain what happens in a food chain when one link is lost.
- Level 3: Ask how the loss of one consumer affects the energy flow.

**Answer: B) The grass will grow more**

## Stage 4: Practice
**Objective:** Reinforce concepts using independent problem-solving with scaffolding

**Problem 1: Food Chain Logic**

In the Pacific coast ecosystem, the following animals live together:

1. Seaweed (producer)
2. Crab (herbivore)
3. Seagull (carnivore) 
4. Seal (carnivore)

Which correctly shows a **food chain** in this ecosystem?

A) Seaweed → Crab → Seagull → Seal
B) Seal → Seagull → Crab → Seaweed
C) Crab → Seaweed → Seagull → Seal
D) Seagull → Crab → Seaweed → Seal

**Intervention Hints:**
- Level 1: Ask: "What eats seaweed? Which animal is a consumer of seaweed?"
- Level 2: Show a diagram of a simple food chain (like grass → rabbit → fox) to illustrate order.
- Level 3: Ask: "Which animal is the first consumer, and who eats that animal?"

**Answer: A) Seaweed → Crab → Seagull → Seal**

**Problem 2: What Happens to Energy Flow?**

In a forest ecosystem, an oak tree dies and falls to the ground. 

What happens to the oak's energy?

A) It goes into the air as heat
B) It stops being used
C) It gets passed to decomposers and then back into the soil
D) It is stored as wood for animals

**Intervention Hints:**
- Level 1: Ask: "What happens to dead things in nature?"
- Level 2: Explain the role of decomposers in recycling nutrients.
- Level 3: Ask: "Who breaks dead things apart to release nutrients back to nature?"

**Answer: C) It gets passed to decomposers and then back into the soil**

**Problem 3: Human Impact**

A city has been built along the edge of a grassland habitat, and the grassland is shrinking. What is the likely effect on the animals in that area?

A) Animals will stay the same
B) Animals will move to a new location
C) Animals will have more food because of the city
D) Animals will have less space to live, fewer places to hide, and fewer resources

**Intervention Hints:**
- Level 1: Ask: "If a grassland gets smaller, how does that affect the animals that live there?"
- Level 2: Show real-world examples of animals being impacted by habitat loss.
- Level 3: Ask: "What happens to an animal's shelter if their habitat shrinks?"

**Answer: D) Animals will have less space to live, fewer places to hide, and fewer resources**

## Stage 5: Capstone
**Objective:** Solve an open-ended, real-world ecosystem problem with multiple steps

**Problem: The Changing Boreal Forest**

The boreal forest in northern Canada is home to many species, including the woodland caribou, wolves, and several types of birds. However, a recent oil and gas project has started in this forest.

1. What type of role does the caribou play in the ecosystem?
A) Producer  
B) Decomposer
C) Consumer
D) Nutrient

2. Some birds in the area depend on seeds from old-growth trees. If the trees are cut down, what happens to these bird populations?
A) They have more space to live
B) They have fewer seeds to eat, so they may leave or decrease in number
C) They start to eat trees instead of seeds
D) They will eat other birds

3. If the number of wolves increases, what is one likely result for the caribou population?
A) The caribou population will increase  
B) The caribou population will stay the same
C) The caribou population will decrease
D) The caribou will move to a new area

**Intervention Hints:**
- Level 1: Ask: "How do we know if an animal eats plants, animals, or both?"
- Level 2: Discuss relationships between predator and prey.
- Level 3: Ask students to consider a broader ecosystem impact of each change.

**Answer:**
1. C) Consumer
2. B) They have fewer seeds to eat, so they may leave or decrease in number
3. C) The caribou population will decrease
