## Stage 1 — Hook

**Hook Story Title:**  
*The Great Bake-Off Mystery*

**Story Summary:**  
In a small Canadian town, the annual community bake-off is coming up! Emma and her friend Mia are preparing their favourite cookies. Emma’s cookies are golden brown and smell amazing — they’re ready! Mia’s cookies seem raw and pale. Their science teacher, Mr. Chen, says they should check what happened in the oven. The mystery deepens when they realize that one of the ingredients — baking soda — has turned a different colour! Why did the cookies turn out differently? Is it because of the baking soda? Is this a good change or a bad one? What’s really happening in this kitchen?

**Narrative Focus:**  
In this story, the change in the cookies (browning, texture change, colour) represents **what happens during chemical and physical changes**. Baking soda being used in a reaction is a key hint that it’s a chemical change. The question of “What really happened?” sets up curiosity.

**Discussion Questions (for child):**
1. Do the cookies in the oven change into something new, or are they still cookies?
2. Is the baking of the cookies a physical or chemical change?
3. What does it mean for a chemical change to happen?

## Stage 2 — Concept

**Concept Introduction:**  
In this lesson, we will learn about the difference between physical and chemical changes when matter changes.

- **Physical Change:** The substance changes form but **stays the same inside** (like ice melting or paper tearing). These changes can be reversed.
- **Chemical Change:** The substance becomes **a new thing** (like iron rusting or food cooking). You can’t easily undo it.

**Key Signs of a Chemical Change:**  
When something changes chemically, we can see signs like:
1. A colour change
2. Heat or light being produced
3. Gas being made
4. A new solid forming (called a precipitate)

**Cultural Note (for Canadian context):**
In Canada, many people cook using ingredients that involve chemical changes: for example, using yeast in bread or baking soda for crispy cookies. These are great examples of everyday chemical changes!

**Comprehension Check:**  
**Question:** Which is a chemical change?  
A. Ice melting into water  
B. Paper being torn into pieces  
C. Bread baking in the oven  
D. A balloon being squished  

**Answer:** C. Bread baking in the oven  

**Intervention Hints (for comprehension check):**  
- **Level 1:** “Is the bread still bread? Or did it become something else?”  
- **Level 2:** “Look at the ingredients — do we still have the same stuff after baking?”  
- **Level 3:** “Which one makes a new thing? What is it now?”

## Stage 3 — Guided

**Problem:**  
Which of these is a good example of a chemical change?  

A. A rock being crushed into powder  
B. Metal being bent into a shape  
C. A leaf falling and rotting  
D. Water freezing into ice  

**Correct Answer:** C. A leaf falling and rotting  

**Explanation:**  
When a leaf rots, it changes into a different substance (the leaf becomes compost). This is a chemical change.  
A, B, and D are physical changes because the substance stays the same, even though it may change shape or form.

**Intervention Hints:**  
- **Level 1:** “Did the leaf become something else, or is it still the same leaf?”  
- **Level 2:** “Can you undo the changes? Was the leaf able to become a leaf again?”  
- **Level 3:** “Look at the signs of change: did the leaf break down and become compost?”  

**Distractors (with misconceptions):**  
A. **A rock being crushed into powder** — Many students confuse this with chemical changes because the size changes.  
B. **Metal being bent into a shape** — Another physical change — students often forget that shape changes don’t create new substances.  
D. **Water freezing into ice** — Students may think this is a chemical change because of the change in form.

## Stage 4 — Practice

### Problem A: What signs would tell us that a chemical change has happened in a science experiment?  

A. Temperature rises and a gas is produced  
B. The container shakes  
C. A solid is added  
D. Light is added to make something glow  

**Correct Answer:** A. Temperature rises and a gas is produced  

**Common Misconceptions:**  
- **Option B:** Confuses signs of motion or energy being applied with chemical changes  
- **Option C:** Simply adding a solid is not evidence of a chemical change  
- **Option D:** Light is not a sign (though it can occur during some chemical reactions, it’s not a standard indication)  

**Intervention Hints:**  
- **Level 1:** “What do you see that tells you something **new** was made?”  
- **Level 2:** “Is this something that happens in a baking soda volcano reaction?”  
- **Level 3:** “If you see heat or gas, what kind of change happened?”  

---

### Problem B: Your mom makes pancakes, and one spills and burns in the pan. Which of these best describes what happened?  

A. It was a physical change  
B. It was a chemical change  
C. Nothing changed  
D. It was a phase change  

**Correct Answer:** B. It was a chemical change  

**Explanation:**  
When food burns, it turns into ash or carbon and creates a new substance. This can’t be undone easily — a chemical change.  

**Intervention Hints:**  
- **Level 1:** “Was the pancake still a pancake, or did it become a new thing (like charcoal)?”  
- **Level 2:** “What would happen if you tried to reverse the burning?”  
- **Level 3:** “If it turns into something black and solid, what kind of change is it?”  

---

### Problem C: Which is an example of a physical change?  

A. Rust forming on a metal fence  
B. A candle burning  
C. Ice melting  
D. Food fermenting  

**Correct Answer:** C. Ice melting  

**Explanation:**  
Melting ice still remains water. It did not change into something new — so it is a **physical change**.  

**Common Misconceptions:**  
A. **Rust forming on a metal fence** — Often confused with physical changes because rust can be scraped off but still involves a chemical process.  
B. **A candle burning** — Burning creates light and heat and leaves ash. This is definitely a chemical change.  
D. **Food fermenting** — This involves the creation of new substances.  

**Intervention Hints:**  
- **Level 1:** “Did it become the same thing again? Is it still ice?”  
- **Level 2:** “Is the ice able to come back to being ice?”  
- **Level 3:** “Does this change create a new material or still the same one? What can be undone easily?”

## Stage 5 — Capstone

**Capstone Problem:**  

Sarah is playing in her backyard and accidentally drops her bicycle tire. It pops and releases air. Then, she puts it back on the bike and rides it around the neighbourhood.  

What are the changes that occurred in the tire, and which category do they fall in?

1. The tire deflated due to a pop → What kind of change?  
2. The tire was placed back on the bike and inflated → What kind of change?  

**Question A:**  
The tire deflated due to a pop → What kind of change?  
A. Chemical  
B. Physical  
C. Phase  
D. Biological  

**Correct Answer:** B. Physical  

**Explanation:**  
Inflating and deflating a tire changes its shape, but the air and tire material are the same — this is a physical change.  

**Question B:**  
The tire was placed back on the bike and inflated → What kind of change?  
A. Not a change  
B. Chemical  
C. Physical  
D. Biological  

**Correct Answer:** C. Physical  

**Explanation:**  
Inflating the tire fills it with air — that changes its shape, but no new substance is formed. Another physical change.  

**Intervention Hints (Between first and second attempt):**  
- Question A: “Did the rubber and air change into something else? Or was it still the same stuff?”  
- Question B: “Did something new (in the tire) appear, or did it still stay the same?”  

**Feedback (if student passes):**  
"You just thought through two changes in a tire and figured out if they were chemical or physical. This shows how good you understand how changes happen to matter. You have a real science skill — the ability to think logically about changes. That’s great!"

**Feedback (if student fails after 2 attempts):**  
"This one had two steps, and that’s hard. It’s okay — you’re learning a lot today. Let’s come back to it next time — you’ve already learned a lot about physical and chemical changes. We’ll practice again!"

It also ties into cooking in Canadian households (especially with ingredients that react like baking soda or yeast), which gives students real-life relevance to their science topics.
