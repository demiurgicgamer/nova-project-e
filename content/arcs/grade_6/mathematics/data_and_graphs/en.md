## Stage 1: Hook  
### Story: Canada's Weather in Numbers  

It's January in Vancouver, and the weather is cold and snowy. In the morning, a science teacher brings a chart to the class showing temperature data over a week. "We've been collecting the high temperatures every day for a week," she says. "Let's take a look."  

The chart shows the temperatures for the days:
- Monday: 3°C  
- Tuesday: 0°C  
- Wednesday: -2°C  
- Thursday: 1°C  
- Friday: 4°C  
- Saturday: 5°C  
- Sunday: 2°C  

"Now, you see the chart?" the teacher asks. "Which day had the highest temperature? And which day had the lowest? Do you notice any patterns in the temperatures over the week?"  

The children start to make observations, but suddenly a student raises her hand and wonders, "How do we even know which graph is the best way to show this data?"  

"That's a really good question," says the teacher. "We use graphs to help us understand and compare data. Today, we're going to learn how."  

**End of Hook**

## Stage 2: Concept  
### Objective: Learn to read and create bar graphs, line graphs, and pie charts.  

**Comprehension Check:**  

**Question:**  
In a bar graph showing the number of pets students own, what does the height of each bar represent?  

**A.** The type of pet  
**B.** The number of students who own that pet  
**C.** The number of pets per student  
**D.** The color of the pet  

✅ Correct Answer: B  

**Interventions:**  
- **Level 1:** "Think of a bar graph like a story — each bar tells you how many times something happened. What does each bar count?"  
- **Level 2:** "A bar graph helps us compare how many of each thing. So, if one bar is tall and another is short, what does that show us about the values?"  
- **Level 3:** "In your graph, what do you think the bar heights show — how many people or how many pets?"

## Stage 3: Guided  
### Problem:  

**Question:**  
A survey was done to find out how many hours students spent reading each day. Here are the results:  

| Hours Spent Reading | Number of Students |
|---------------------|--------------------|
| 0–1 hour            | 5                  |
| 1–2 hours           | 8                  |
| 2–3 hours           | 3                  |
| 3–4 hours           | 4                  |

Which type of graph would best show this data?  

**A.** Bar graph  
**B.** Pie chart  
**C.** Line graph  
**D.** Line plot  

✅ Correct Answer: A  

**Interventions:**  
- **Level 1:** "What kind of data are we showing? Is it amounts that can be compared and grouped like here?"  
- **Level 2:** "Look at what the data is — we have categories (hours) and numbers of students. What kind of chart shows categories and how many in each category?"  
- **Level 3:** "We want to show how many students read how many hours. We don't want a smooth line or show parts of a whole — what chart best shows this grouping?"

## Stage 4: Practice  

### Problem 1:  
**Question:**  
A line graph shows the number of students at a school from 2015 to 2020. In 2018, there were 450 students. Which statement is best supported by a line graph?  

**A.** In 2018, there were more students than in 2016  
**B.** The school had 450 students in 2018  
**C.** The most students were in 2020  
**D.** The school’s student number went up every year  

✅ Correct Answer: B  

**Interventions:**  
- **Level 1:** "A line graph shows changes over time. What can we read directly from a point on the graph?"  
- **Level 2:** "If we see a point at 2018 with a height of 450, what does that number tell us?"  
- **Level 3:** "We are told that there were 450 students in 2018. Is this a direct value we can read? What else is true?"  

---

### Problem 2:  
**Question:**  
A pie chart shows the favorite lunch foods of students in Grade 6. If 30% of the students like sandwiches, and there are 60 students in total, how many students like sandwiches?  

**A.** 18  
**B.** 20  
**C.** 30  
**D.** 15  

✅ Correct Answer: A  

**Interventions:**  
- **Level 1:** "If 30% of 60 students like sandwiches, think of 30% as 30 out of 100. So how many is that of 60?"  
- **Level 2:** "You can find 30% of 60 by multiplying 0.3 by 60. Try that and see."  
- **Level 3:** "We know there are 60 students. If 30% of them like sandwiches, how many students is that?"  

---

### Problem 3:  
**Question:**  
A class of students made a bar graph showing how many books they read last month. The tallest bar represents 15 books. Which of these conclusions is correct about the data?  

**A.** 15 students read that many books  
**B.** 15 students read the least number of books  
**C.** The students read a total of 15 books  
**D.** The tallest bar shows 15 books were read  

✅ Correct Answer: D  

**Interventions:**  
- **Level 1:** "In a bar graph, what does each bar represent?"  
- **Level 2:** "If a bar goes up to 15, does that mean 15 people read 15 books? Or what?"  
- **Level 3:** "What is the tallest bar showing — the number of books read, or the number of students?"

## Stage 5: Capstone  

### Final Problem:  
**Question:**  
The table below shows how many hours students study on a typical school night.  

| Hours Studied | Number of Students |
|---------------|--------------------|
| 0–1 hour      | 4                  |
| 1–2 hours     | 6                  |
| 2–3 hours     | 8                  |
| 3–4 hours     | 3                  |

**Part A:** What type of graph would be best to show this data?  
**Part B:** If the teacher wants to create a pie chart based on this information, what percentage of students studied 2–3 hours?  

**A.** Part A: Line graph; Part B: 30%  
**B.** Part A: Bar graph; Part B: 33%  
**C.** Part A: Pie chart; Part B: 25%  
**D.** Part A: Bar graph; Part B: 40%  

✅ Correct Answer: D  

**Interventions (between first and second attempts only):**  
- **Guide Question:** "How many total students are in the class based on the table?"  
- **Guidance:** "After you know the total, think about how many studied 2–3 hours, and then work out the percentage."  
- **Follow-up:** "What does the total help us do with the percentage?"  

**If Passed:**  
> "Amazing! You read data from a table and decided how to best display it, while also calculating a percentage. You’ve made some smart math connections — great job."  

**If Failed After 2 Attempts:**  
> "That's really hard to solve in two steps, which is perfect for advanced learners like you. Let’s come back to data like this next time — you've learned so much, we’ll build on it!"
