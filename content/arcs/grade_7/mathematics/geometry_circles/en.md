## Stage 1: Hook

In this stage, the child is introduced to circles in context and is motivated to want to know more.

**Hook Story:**

You're going to build a new playground on your school's land – and part of it is going to need a circular sandbox with a diameter of 12 feet. Your supervisor wants to make sure it's the right size, so she asks you: "How far would you walk around the edge of that sandbox?" This measurement is the **circumference** of the circle. Then she asks: "How much sand would fit in it?" That would be the **area** of the circle. 

But here’s the kicker – your supervisor is also wondering how many times a wheel from a school bus (with a diameter of 2.5 feet) would need to rotate to cover the full length of that 12-foot diameter sandbox’s edge. This is a real-world problem in motion, and to solve it, we need to know the circumference and be able to use the constant π.  

Wouldn't it be great to learn how to calculate these? This is the power of understanding circles!

**Culture angle 1:** The idea of measurement being practical in a school environment.

**Culture angle 2:** Using the same concept (wheel rotation) as in the French "pièce de resistance" of the Canadian road system where wheel size and measurements are important for traffic control and vehicle safety.

## Stage 2: Concept

In this stage, a key concept is introduced, explained, and verified.

**Concept Introduction:**

Let’s talk about circles and how they work. A circle has a **center**, and from that center, all points on the edge are the same distance. That distance is the circle’s **radius (r)**. The **diameter (d)** is twice the radius, so d = 2r. 

When we go around the edge of a circle, that distance is called the **circumference** (C). If you could stretch that edge out straight, the circumference would be longer than the diameter.

There’s this special number, **π (pi)**, that helps us find the circumference. No matter what size the circle is, the ratio of its circumference to its diameter is always the same. That number is about 3.14159 and is written as π.

The **formula** for **circumference** is:
**C = πd** or **C = 2πr**

The **area** of a circle is found using:
**A = πr²**

In real life, circles are found everywhere – car wheels, pizza, clocks, garden hoses, and even the rings of Saturn.

Let’s start by trying to find out how much distance we walk when we walk around the edge of our sandbox, and how much space it takes up.

**Comprehension check:**

*The diameter of a circle is 6 meters. What is the radius?*

- A) 12 meters
- B) 3 meters
- C) 6 meters
- D) 1.5 meters

**Intervention hints:**

- Level 1 (Basic vocabulary): "Think of a circle’s diameter and radius like a line from center to edge – how does that compare to a line all the way across?"
- Level 2 (Formula recall): "Which is twice as big: the radius or the diameter?"
- Level 3 (Conceptual understanding): "If your diameter is 6 meters, what is exactly half of that?"

## Stage 3: Guided

In this stage, students practice applying the concept with support and scaffolding.

**Problem:**

You are buying a circular cake for a birthday party. The cake has a radius of 10 inches.

1. What is the **circumference** of the cake?
2. What is the **area** of the cake?

Use π ≈ 3.14.

Let’s tackle the first part together.

1. **C = 2πr**
   C = 2 × 3.14 × 10 = ?
   
2. **A = πr²**
   A = 3.14 × 10² = ?
   
**Solution:**
1. C = 62.8 inches
2. A = 314 square inches

**Guided Problem:**

A circular swimming pool has a radius of 7 feet. What is the **area** of the pool, using π ≈ 3.14?

- A) 153.86 sq ft
- B) 43.96 sq ft
- C) 219.8 sq ft
- D) 112.96 sq ft

**Intervention hints:**

- Level 1 (Basic steps): "You know π and r. What’s the first step to calculate area?"
- Level 2 (Formula recall): "Which formula do you use for area of a circle?"
- Level 3 (Calculation confidence): "Multiply 3.14 by 7, then multiply by 7 again."

## Stage 4: Practice

In this stage, problems are solved independently, with some scaffolding.

**Problem 1:**

A circular garden has a diameter of 14 meters. What is the **circumference** of the garden? (Use π ≈ 3.14)

- A) 153.86 m
- B) 21.98 m
- C) 43.96 m
- D) 61.54 m

**Solution:**
C = πd = 3.14 × 14 = 43.96 m

**Hint levels:**
- Level 1 (Basic concept): "Do you know what formula to use for circumference?"
- Level 2 (Formula recall): "What is the formula? Which one uses diameter?"
- Level 3 (Calculation confidence): "Is this how you do 3.14 × 14?"

---

**Problem 2:**

A pizza has a diameter of 16 inches. What is the **area** of the pizza? (Use π ≈ 3.14)

- A) 50.24 sq in
- B) 200.96 sq in
- C) 100.48 sq in
- D) 502.4 sq in

**Solution:**
Area = πr² = 3.14 × 8² = 3.14 × 64 = 200.96 sq in

**Hint levels:**
- Level 1 (Basic concept): "What do you know about a pizza and its size? What do you need to find?"
- Level 2 (Formula recall): "Which formula do you use for area of a circle?"
- Level 3 (Calculation confidence): "Don’t forget to square the radius first, then multiply by π."

---

**Problem 3:**

A circular fountain has a radius of 5 feet. What is the **circumference** of the fountain? (Use π ≈ 3.14)

- A) 15.7 feet
- B) 31.4 feet
- C) 31.4 feet²
- D) 78.5 feet²

**Solution:**
C = 2πr = 2 × 3.14 × 5 = 31.4 feet

**Hint levels:**
- Level 1 (Formula recall): "Which formula do you use for circumference?"
- Level 2 (Formula recall): "Do you use diameter or radius in the circumference formula?"
- Level 3 (Units confusion): "This is a distance – should it have square units (²)?"

## Stage 5: Capstone

In this stage, students apply the concept in a complex, real-world setting.

**Problem:**

A bicycle wheel has a radius of 14 inches. What is the **distance the wheel would travel** in 5 complete rotations?

First, calculate the **circumference** of the wheel.

Then, multiply that value by 5.

- A) 87.92 inches
- B) 439.6 inches
- C) 879.2 inches
- D) 100.48 inches

**Solution:**
C = 2πr = 2 × 3.14 × 14 = 87.92 inches
Distance = 87.92 × 5 = 439.6 inches

**Intervention guidance:**

- Between first and second attempt:
  **"What is the circumference formula? What does it tell us? What do we do next with five rotations?"**

**If passed:**

> "You just solved a real-world problem using the circumference formula – that’s a strong indicator you’ve really got the hang of it! Well done."

**If failed after two attempts:**

> "This one took a few steps and it's completely okay if it was challenging. You’re off to a good start – come back to it next time and we’ll review!"

## Stage 5: Capstone (Alternative)

**Problem (Creative Application):**

A circular garden with diameter 20 meters is surrounded by a path 3 meters wide. What is the **area of the path**?

(Hint: The path forms a larger circle around the garden.)

- A) 113.04 sq m
- B) 138.16 sq m
- C) 215.92 sq m
- D) 226.08 sq m

**Solution:**
- Garden radius = 10 m
- Outer radius = 10 m + 3 m = 13 m
- Area of outer circle = π(13)² = 3.14 × 169 = 530.66 sq m
- Area of garden = π(10)² = 3.14 × 100 = 314 sq m
- Area of path = 530.66 − 314 = 216.66 sq m ≈ 215.92 sq m
