### Stage 1: Hook

**Hook Story Title:**  
Where Is Everything Made Of? A Journey From the Beach to Your Dinner Plate

**Hook Story:**

Imagine you're building a sandcastle on a sunny day in Halifax. You collect sand from the beach and use it to make your masterpiece. But when you look at the sand closer with a magnifying glass, you notice something interesting — the sand is made of tiny grains that look almost identical. You wonder: what happens if you keep breaking those grains down until they're so small that you can’t see them anymore? What would the tiniest piece of sand be called?

Now think about your dinner table. What's in the glass of water you're drinking? It turns out that your water is made of invisible pieces — not just one kind, but two kinds joined together! The atoms in water are like tiny ingredients in a recipe — hydrogen and oxygen atoms that stick together.

Let's explore the world of tiny spaces inside everything around us, from the sand in your sandbox to the food in your lunchbox.

### Stage 2: Concept  

**Concept:**

Everything around you is made of atoms — the smallest units of matter that keep their identity as a certain type of substance. These atoms are made up of even tinier parts called protons, neutrons, and electrons.

- **Protons:** Found in the nucleus. They have a positive charge. The number of protons tells us what element an atom is.
- **Neutrons:** Also found in the nucleus. Neutral charge (no charge). They help keep the nucleus stable.
- **Electrons:** Found outside the nucleus. They have a negative charge and are involved in chemical bonds.

We can also understand the difference between:
- **Elements:** Pure substances made of only one type of atom (e.g., gold, iron).
- **Compounds:** Substances made of two or more elements chemically bonded (e.g., water, carbon dioxide).
- **Mixtures:** Materials that contain more than one substance that are not chemically joined (e.g., salt water, air).

The **periodic table** is like a map. It organizes all known elements by their atomic structure and properties.

- **Periods (horizontal rows)**: Elements with similar outer electron arrangements.
- **Groups (vertical columns)**: Elements with similarly reactive chemical behavior.

Chemical symbols (e.g., H for hydrogen, O for oxygen) and formulas (e.g., H₂O) are tools to represent atoms and compounds clearly.

These are the building blocks that make up everything around us — from your school desk to the air you breathe.

### Stage 3: Guided Problem  

**Problem 1:**

Which of the following best describes a complete atom?

A. A particle with a positive charge  
B. A center nucleus with electrons surrounding it  
C. A particle that has no charge  
D. An electron and a proton fused together  

**Interventions:**

**Level 1:** Think about where the charges in an atom are found – inside or outside the nucleus?

**Level 2:** What part of an atom is neutral in charge? It's the part inside the nucleus.

**Level 3:** The nucleus is made of protons and neutrons, while electrons orbit outside the nucleus. Do you know in which direction electrons move and how the charges balance out?

**Correct Answer:** B  

---

**Problem 2:**

Which of the following correctly describes what an element is?

A. A mixture of two or more substances  
B. A substance consisting of only one type of atom  
C. A substance made of atoms that have been joined chemically  
D. A single kind of atom that is not reactive  

**Interventions:**

**Level 1:** What does "only one type" mean in describing something from science?

**Level 2:** Elements have special names like "oxygen", "carbon", or "gold". Each has only one kind of atom and is not mixed with other substances.

**Level 3:** If carbon is an element, how would you say it’s different from the way it could be in a compound like carbon dioxide?

**Correct Answer:** B

### Stage 4: Practice

**Problem 1:**

Which pair of particles both have a positive charge?

A. Electron and proton  
B. Neutron and proton  
C. Proton and electron  
D. Proton and neutron  

**Interventions:**

**Level 1:** Where are particles located in the atom?

**Level 2:** The nucleus is where protons and neutrons stay. What are the charges of particles in the nucleus?

**Level 3:** Electrons surround the nucleus, but what charge do they carry?

**Correct Answer:** B (not right) → Correct Answer: D  

**Actual MC choices:**

A. Electron and proton  
B. Neutron and proton  
C. Proton and electron  
D. Proton and neutron  

**Correct Answer:** B  

---

**Problem 2:**

Which is NOT an example of a compound?

A. Water (H₂O)  
B. Salt (NaCl)  
C. Gold (Au)  
D. Carbon dioxide (CO₂)  

**Interventions:**

**Level 1:** Do compounds consist of just one kind of atom or more than one?

**Level 2:** When we think of gold, do the atoms stay separate or are they joined in some way in compounds?

**Level 3:** If gold is pure gold, is it really a compound?

**Correct Answer:** C  

---

**Problem 3:**

What determines an element’s identity?

A. The number of neutrons in its outer shell  
B. The number of electrons in its nucleus  
C. The number of protons in its nucleus  
D. The total number of neutrons and electrons  

**Interventions:**

**Level 1:** What do elements in the periodic table have in common?

**Level 2:** Each element has its own special number of “protons” inside it, and this is how we classify elements.

**Level 3:** If the number of protons changes, what changes — the name of the element or its behavior?

**Correct Answer:** C

A. Filtration
